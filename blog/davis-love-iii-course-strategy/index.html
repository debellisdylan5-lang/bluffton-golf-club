How to Play a Davis Love III Golf Course: Strategy at Bluffton Golf Club

Bluffton Golf Club September 3, 2026 8-minute read

Direct answer

The smartest approach is to choose the correct tee, favor position over maximum distance, respect water and angles, and leave comfortable approach shots.

A strong plan for a Davis Love III golf course starts before the first swing. Bluffton Golf Club is an 18-hole, par-71 layout with six tee options: Champion (Gold) 6,692 yards; Pro (Blue) 6,379; Member (White) 6,005; Hybrid (W/G) 5,722; Club (Green) 5,559; Forward (Red) 4,897. Its Lowcountry setting—framed by mature oaks and pines, with lagoons and other water features—makes thoughtful decisions more valuable than simply pursuing maximum distance.

The following guidance is intentionally strategic rather than hole-specific. It does not assume exact landing areas, green shapes, or hazards on individual holes. Instead, it provides a repeatable framework golfers can use while reviewing each shot in front of them.

Start With Strategic Tee Selection

The scorecard yardage is only one part of choosing tees. A suitable set should let you hit a realistic variety of approach clubs, reach appropriate landing areas with controlled swings, and occasionally consider a green in regulation after a solid drive. If most approaches require your longest clubs, moving forward can create a more complete and enjoyable test.

At BGC, six tee options provide meaningful flexibility: Champion (Gold) at 6,692 yards, Pro (Blue) at 6,379, Member (White) at 6,005, Hybrid (W/G) at 5,722, Club (Green) at 5,559, and Forward (Red) at 4,897. Select tees according to dependable carry distance—not the longest drive you have hit—and consider how weather, turf conditions, and current form may change effective yardage.

A practical pre-round check

  • Use your normal carry distance rather than total distance on unusually firm ground.
  • Consider whether forced carries are comfortable with your typical shot.
  • Choose a yardage that lets you play more than fairway woods and hybrids into greens.
  • Move forward when conditions make the course play longer; tee choice is a strategy decision, not a status decision.

Play for Angles, Not Just Distance

Good course management asks two questions from the tee: Where is the widest safe landing area? and From which position will the next shot be most manageable? The longest available club is not automatically the best answer.

A shorter club can improve control, keep water out of play, or leave a fuller approach yardage. Conversely, driver can be the correct strategic choice when its extra carry meaningfully expands the next-shot options. Evaluate the complete sequence rather than treating each swing as an isolated challenge.

When a water feature influences one side, aim points and shot shape matter. Give the hazard enough respect that your normal dispersion—not merely your intended line—has room to finish safely. Favoring a playable angle is different from chasing a perfect angle that requires an uncomfortably precise tee shot.

Make Risk–Reward Decisions With Clear Math

Risk–reward golf becomes easier when you define both sides of the decision. What do you gain by taking on the demanding line? What is the likely cost of a typical miss? A good gamble offers a meaningful advantage and suits a shot you can execute with confidence.

Before committing, compare three outcomes: the result of a well-played aggressive shot, the result of your common miss, and the result of the conservative option. If aggression only saves a small amount of yardage but brings water or a difficult recovery into your normal dispersion, position is usually more valuable.

Score context matters, too. A golfer protecting a good round may choose differently from one seeking a late opportunity. The important step is to make that choice before standing over the ball.

Choose Clubs Backward From the Approach

Effective club selection begins at the target and works backward. Identify a comfortable approach distance, then choose the tee club that most reliably creates it. For one player, that may mean leaving a full wedge. Another may prefer a controlled short iron rather than a partial shot.

Use carry yardage as the foundation around lagoons and water features. Total distance can vary with turf and landing angle, but the ball must first cover everything between you and the target. When conditions or confidence make the required carry uncertain, select a different target, a different club, or a safer route.

Build a small on-course yardage system

  • Know stock carry numbers for every club.
  • Keep reliable three-quarter wedge distances.
  • Adjust for wind and temperature without abandoning your normal tempo.
  • When between clubs, choose according to the safer miss rather than ego.

Manage the Miss Before You Swing

Every golfer has a shot pattern. Smart golf strategy in Bluffton, SC, accounts for the full pattern instead of planning around perfect contact. Identify the trouble that creates the largest penalty, then select a target that gives your common miss more room.

A safe miss is not always the side closest to the flag. It is the area that preserves a clear next shot and reduces the chance of compounding an error. When water is relevant, consider both direction and distance: a pulled shot and an unexpectedly long shot may carry different consequences.

After a poor swing, reset the plan. The recovery goal is usually to restore position, not immediately recover the lost stroke. One disciplined decision can prevent a single mistake from becoming a large number.

Account for Lowcountry Wind and Weather

Lowcountry conditions can change how a course plays from one round to the next. Wind may be felt differently in exposed areas than among mature oaks and pines, while temperature and moisture can affect carry and rollout. These are general regional considerations; golfers should assess the conditions they encounter on the day.

Watch treetops, flags, and the flight of playing partners’ shots. Into the wind, use enough club and make a balanced swing rather than trying to overpower the shot. Downwind, remember that a flatter landing angle may create more release. Crosswinds call for a starting line that provides room for both expected movement and your natural dispersion.

Weather can also alter tee suitability. A set that fits perfectly on a calm, warm day may play considerably longer in cool, damp, or windy conditions. Bluffton Golf Club’s six tee options make it possible to adjust thoughtfully.

Why Repeat Rounds Reveal More Strategy

The first round provides visual information; later rounds add evidence. You begin to learn which clubs reliably place the ball in comfortable positions, where conservative choices genuinely save strokes, and which risks fit your own game.

Keep brief notes after playing: tee clubs that created preferred approach distances, wind directions that changed club selection, and situations where the safe target was not obvious from the first look. Repeat rounds turn those observations into a personal strategy guide.

This is one of the enduring attractions of strategic course design. The challenge is not limited to execution. It also asks golfers to interpret terrain, understand angles, manage uncertainty, and choose the right shot for their abilities that day.
